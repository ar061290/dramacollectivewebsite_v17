I don’t remember the first character I loved.1

I remember the first one I couldn’t stand.2

Everything about Higgins (yes, him, if you know, you know) irritated me. His decisions seemed selfish. His reactions felt exaggerated. His priorities made no sense.

So when I played him, I made him exactly what I thought he was.

Selfish.

Difficult.

Wrong.

The performance was technically fine.

It was also terrible.

The character felt flat because I had already decided who he was. I wasn’t playing Higgins. I was playing my judgment of him.

So I started over.3

Instead of asking, “How do I make this character seem selfish?” I asked, “Why would this person think what they’re doing is reasonable?”

The question changed everything.

Suddenly, the pauses meant something.

The defensiveness meant something.

The decisions I had dismissed as irrational began to make sense within the world the character inhabited.

I still didn’t agree with Higgins.

That was the important part.

I had always assumed that understanding someone required agreement. Theatre taught me something more useful: understanding and agreement are completely different things.

You can understand a character’s motivation and still dislike their choices.

Once I realized that, I started noticing how rarely I gave real people the same courtesy.

Someone would annoy me, and I would construct an explanation almost instantly.

They’re arrogant.

They’re lazy.

They don’t care.

The label felt like an answer because it ended the investigation.

Theatre made me want to keep investigating. I think it ultimately made me more emphatic, without even realizing it.

I started thinking of my judgments as hypotheses rather than conclusions.

“Maybe I’m right.”

“Maybe I’m missing something.”

“What would have to be true for this person’s behavior to make sense?”

That question hasn’t made me endlessly patient or perfectly understanding. I still judge people. I still get annoyed. I still get things wrong. Like any other person, of course.

But now I am more interested in finding out WHY I might be wrong.

That has changed the way I approach both theatre and life.

When I tried organizing a recreation of Heathers, I noticed signs of students being able to transform in ways I couldn’t have predicted. Someone quiet in class could become magnetic onstage. Someone who insisted they “weren’t an actor” could become completely absorbed in a character. Someone who seemed confident could suddenly become terrified when all attention turned toward them.

People are more complicated than the versions of them I, and sometimes they themselves construct from a distance.

So are characters.

The character I hated taught me that my first interpretation is rarely the whole story.

I think that is why I love theatre even more now.

Every script begins with words on a page.

Then someone asks the most important question:

Why?
