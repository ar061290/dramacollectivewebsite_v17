The ‘You’ Inside of You Doesn’t Exist


There is something strange about spending years pretending to be other people and then realizing you don't completely know who you are when you aren't pretending.

I started theatre when I was five. Since then, I've been given countless characters, each with different voices, movements, fears and ambitions. I learned how to walk differently, speak differently, occupy a room differently.

Eventually I became interested in the boundary between acting and authenticity.

A character is constructed.

So am I.

At school, I know how I am expected to behave (whether I follow that or not is a different question). In a classroom, I am a student. During STEM Racing, I am an engineer. In a rehearsal room, I am an actor. When I start something, I am a leader.

None of these versions of me feels fake.

But none of them is the whole picture either.

That used to bother me.

I thought authenticity meant discovering some single, permanent version of myself underneath all the roles.

Theatre made that idea harder to believe.

When I play a character, I don't become less myself. I actually discover things about myself that I might never have noticed without the character as an excuse.

I can make a character angry and discover that I understand that anger.

I can make someone vulnerable and discover that I am more comfortable with vulnerability when I don't have to call it mine.

I can say something onstage that I would never think to say as myself.

The strange part is that pretending can sometimes reveal more truth than trying to be authentic.

That realization has changed how I think about identity.

I no longer see my different interests as competing versions of me—the physics student versus the theatre enthusiast versus the engineer. They are different ways of exploring the same person.

Theatre lets me explore people from the inside.

Engineering lets me explore systems from the inside.

Both require me to temporarily adopt a perspective that isn't my own and ask what happens if I take it seriously.

Maybe that is why I have never been particularly interested in choosing between the different parts of myself.

I don't think identity is a role we eventually finish rehearsing.

It is closer to an ensemble.

Different versions enter at different moments, with different people. Some become louder. Some disappear for a while. Some surprise you when they return.

And perhaps the point isn't to figure out which one is the “real” me, because does a true self even exist? I don't think so.

Perhaps what's more important is to keep listening closely enough to recognize all of those voices.
