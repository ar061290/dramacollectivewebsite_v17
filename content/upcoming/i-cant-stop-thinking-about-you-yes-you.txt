I Can’t Stop Thinking About You. Yes, You

I have never been particularly good at leaving people alone in my head.

Give me a stranger's reaction, a friend's silence, or a character's inexplicable decision, and my brain immediately starts looking for the mechanism behind it.

Theatre gave that habit somewhere to go.

I started formal drama training when I was five, long before I could have explained why I liked it. At first, theatre was simply doing things I normally wouldn't do: speaking too loudly, moving too dramatically, becoming someone who wasn't me. But as I grew older, I became less interested in performing a character than in figuring one out.

Why would she say that?

Why did he hesitate?

What happened before this scene?

What would this person consider a completely reasonable decision?

I began treating characters almost like experiments. I would change a pause, alter the way I entered a room, deliver the same line with a different motivation, and watch how the entire scene changed. A character who seemed cruel in one interpretation could become frightened in another. Someone who appeared confident could suddenly look defensive if I changed a single movement.

Eventually, I realized that I wasn't really learning how to act.

I was learning how to ask *why*.

That habit followed me offstage.

I began noticing how easily I constructed explanations for people. Someone was arrogant. Someone was lazy. Someone was quiet because they didn't care. The explanation arrived before I had even realized I was making one.

Theatre made me suspicious of those explanations.

When I play a character, I cannot simply decide that she is selfish and stop there. I have to understand why she believes her actions make sense. That doesn't mean agreeing with her. In fact, some of the most interesting characters are the ones whose choices I still dislike after I understand them.

I began trying to extend that distinction to real people.

Understanding is not approval. It is curiosity.

That realization became particularly important when I helped create start the CHIREC Drama Collective. I expected people to join because they loved acting. Instead, I found students who wanted to experiment, students who were terrified of performing, students who wanted somewhere to be loud, and students who seemed to become entirely different people once they were given a character.

I started paying attention not only to what people performed, but to what changed when they were given permission to perform.

Theatre, I discovered, is a strange way of studying humanity. You can spend weeks examining a fictional person and then realize that the questions you were asking about them were questions you had been asking about yourself all along.

Why do I react like that?

What am I protecting?

Which parts of me are choices, and which are habits?

I still love the technical side of theatre—the blocking, the timing, the construction of a scene—but what keeps me there is the uncertainty (and yes, that's an em dash, and as someone who learnt basic punctuation in middle school, I'm a proud em dash user)

A physics problem usually asks me to find the answer.

A character asks me to understand the question.

And people rarely come with an answer key.

That is precisely what I like about them...and sometimes detest :)

That's why I wanted to be a part of starting the Drama Collective.
